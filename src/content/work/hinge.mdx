## What we tested, and who took part

Hinge sells itself on intent. The motto is "Designed to be Deleted," and the idea underneath it is that the app works well enough that you stop needing it. We focused on where that experience begins: signing up, before you've seen a single match.

Ten people, aged 18 to 29, which is where most of the dating app population lives. Eight had never used Hinge. Two had used it and been inactive for six months or more, which gave us a useful second angle: people who already knew the conventions.

Sessions ran over Zoom with people sharing their own phone screens, thirty to forty-five minutes each, thinking out loud, with one moderator and at least one notetaker every time. Participants could use their real details or a test account we set up, whichever felt more comfortable, since signup asks for personal details like religion.

Three tasks. **Sign up and build a profile. Go back and change an answer you already gave. Find and adjust Dating Preferences and Dealbreakers.**

Afterwards we each coded every session separately, gave each person their own colour of sticky note, and worked through it together on a Miro board until three findings emerged.

## The good news first

Worth saying up front, because it shapes everything after it: once people found a feature, all ten of them could use it. Questions got answered, toggles got toggled, preferences got adjusted. The interactions themselves worked well.

So the story here isn't about a confusing interface. It's about finding things, going back, and knowing what you're able to change.

## Nobody could complete task two

Task two seemed like the simplest thing we asked: go back and change an answer you already gave.

**Ten out of ten couldn't.** By the definition we wrote down before the first session, that counts as a failure: unable to complete the task.

As we looked closer, it came down to one missing button. Hinge's signup runs past twenty questions, one per screen, covering your age, your pronouns, your politics and your religion. None of those screens has a back button. Once an answer is in, you can't change it until you've finished the whole flow.

It also turned out to be the finding the rest of our notes kept connecting back to.

<Pull attr="Participant 3">
  You can't, like, go back and adjust your preferences as you're doing it initially. You have to go through the whole onboarding and then adjust it later, which I think is... a little goofy.
</Pull>

Skipping came up as a theme of its own. Two people wanted to skip questions, and only one of them found out how. Three didn't realise they had to finish every required question before they could look around at all, which means sharing details like your politics before you get to see whether the app is right for you.

## Same word, opposite meaning

People don't usually notice a missing back button as a missing button. They notice being stuck with a mistake they can already see. Participants described the flow as long and rigid, and a few realised their only way to fix a wrong answer was to delete the account and start over.

One participant put the next step into words.

<Pull attr="Participant 2, after realising they couldn't fix an answer">
  I'd probably close out the app and restart... I'd probably delete the app.
</Pull>

**This is where the pieces came together.** Hinge's brand is built on the idea that you'll delete the app because it worked. One of the strongest reactions across ten sessions was someone reaching for that same word before they'd finished signing up, for the opposite reason. It's the part I'd share first with a product team.

A back button in a linear form is a fairly small change. What it protects is a new member's very first experience with the app, at the point where they've invested the least and it's easiest to walk away.

## Three findings, and the trade-offs in each fix

Each fix comes with trade-offs, and they're worth looking at alongside the recommendations.

<Decision num="01" title="Signup only moves forward" didLabel="Recommended" costLabel="The trade-off">
  <Fragment slot="finding">All ten failed the task of changing an answer. Five of ten separately described the flow as overwhelming. Four of ten were frustrated by pop-ups interrupting it. Two hit bugs that made them close and reopen the app mid-signup.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">A back control on every signup screen, opposite the next arrow so the bar reads as a pair. Top priority, with ten of ten people affected.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Relying on people editing answers after signup. They can, but it isn't obvious that they can, and it doesn't help with the mistake they can see right now.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">Signup is a funnel, and every control you add is another way out of the current step. A back button does make it easier to move away from that screen. We think that's a smaller risk than people leaving the app entirely.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="02" title="Dealbreakers are spread out, and the explanation is easy to miss" didLabel="Recommended" costLabel="The trade-off">
  <Fragment slot="finding">Six of ten couldn't find Dating Preferences, which sits behind a small unlabelled icon in the corner. Five of ten couldn't find Dealbreakers, which aren't in one place but inside individual preference screens. Four of ten weren't sure what a Dealbreaker does. One thought they were tiebreakers for choosing between similar profiles.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Put Dealbreakers in one place, increase the contrast so an active one looks active, and label the preferences icon.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Adding more explanatory copy next to the feature. The explanation is already on screen, as the trade-off below describes.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">Hinge already explains this. "Dealbreakers will limit who you see and who sees you" is right there on screen, and all four people who misunderstood the feature had it in front of them. If the words aren't being read, adding more words won't fix it.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="03" title="It's hard to tell what's free until you reach the paywall" didLabel="Recommended" costLabel="The trade-off">
  <Fragment slot="finding">Seven of ten didn't know how much was behind the paywall until they reached it. Two found out that their dating intention, which they'd picked for free during signup, couldn't be changed afterwards without paying. One found the daily swipe limit by running out, with no counter or warning beforehand.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Flag the settings that will lock later, show a swipe counter before the limit instead of at it, and let people try the premium filters before they choose.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Leaving discovery to the paywall itself, which is how it works today.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">A paywall that appears at the moment of need can convert well, so earlier warnings might cost some upgrades. What we heard in sessions pointed the other way: people who reached a paywall unexpectedly described the free app as broken rather than basic, and that impression can be hard to change.</Fragment>
</Decision>

Wording was a challenge too. Five weren't sure what some of the terms meant. Two didn't know the difference between monogamy and non-monogamy, and one said they'd have searched for it. Two couldn't tell "life partner" from "long-term relationship." Two found it hard to rate their own politics on a scale with nothing to anchor it. One skipped the gender identity question rather than answer something they weren't sure about.

## Ranking the recommendations

Three findings led to twelve recommendations, which is more than a team would take on at once. So we ranked them by how many of the ten people hit the problem, how much it blocked a basic task, and how clearly it went against a usability principle. Two came out high, six medium and four low.

The ranking is what helps a team decide where to start.

## Why this matters right now

Roughly 3 in 10 US adults have used a dating app, so this is a mainstream experience rather than a niche one. But usage has been declining. About 1.4 million people stopped using dating apps between 2023 and 2024, and 131,000 of those were Hinge users.

Many people point to burnout: the apps start to feel like a chore or like admin, rather than something social. A long signup form with no way back can feel the same way.

Our findings won't change that trend on their own. But making signup feel less like paperwork is a practical, solvable piece of it, and it's the first thing every new member sees.

<div class="caveat">
  <h2 style="margin-block-start:0">What I'd be careful with in this study</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><b>We couldn't measure how long signup takes.</b> One of our main findings is that signup feels long, but not every transcript had timestamps, so we can't say how long it actually takes. That's the biggest gap in the report, and it was ours to prevent.</li>
    <li><b>We recruited through our own networks.</b> Most participants knew somebody on the team, which can affect how openly people share criticism. A few also came in with negative feelings about dating apps, which isn't the same group as people who install one voluntarily.</li>
    <li><b>The sessions weren't identical.</b> We all kept to the core script but didn't all ask the same follow-ups, and people didn't all get the same signup questions; some got the voice prompt and some didn't. The grid looks more uniform than the sessions behind it were.</li>
    <li><b>We changed the recruitment criteria partway through.</b> It was a reasonable change, but it means the second half of the sample was chosen differently from the first.</li>
    <li><b>One returning user did best, largely because she'd used the app before.</b> It's a helpful reminder that much of what we measured is the cost of being new, not only the cost of the design. On a study about the new user experience, that distinction matters.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

**What we delivered:** a report with three findings, twelve recommendations ranked by priority and reach, task outcomes for every participant, and the affinity board behind all of it. This was coursework, so the report wasn't shared with Hinge and the product hasn't changed because of it.

**What I'd do differently:** plot that outcome grid after session three instead of session ten. The scale was in the notes template from day one, which is the only reason the grid exists, but we only looked at it as a whole at the end. If we'd plotted it early, we'd have seen the second row fill in by session three, and we could have used the remaining seven sessions to dig into why people were getting stuck.
