## The brief said K-12. Our first conversations helped us narrow it.

Club for the Future lets K-12 students draw a digital postcard. It flies to space on a New Shepard or New Glenn rocket, comes back with a "Flown to Space" stamp on it, and lands in your inbox. It runs on the website and on touchscreen kiosks in museums, and thousands of kids have done it.

We started out designing for the brief as written, and two expert interviews reshaped it early. Jason Yip from KidsTeam at the UW iSchool, and Julie Kientz, who chairs our department, each made the same point on their own: a six-year-old and a seventeen-year-old need very different things, and designing for both at once tends to serve neither well.

Kientz also shared an idea that ended up guiding the whole visual direction.

<Pull attr="Julie Kientz, Chair, UW Human Centered Design & Engineering">
  Kids prefer interfaces that don't feel like they were made for younger kids, even when they are.
</Pull>

So we focused on ages 8 to 12 and kept the edges covered with a low floor and a high ceiling. Easy to start, with plenty of depth for anyone who goes looking.

Yip also raised something we hadn't considered yet: research with kids under a corporate sponsor needs a clear consent process before any design questions come up. Kientz pointed us to UW Engineering Discovery Days, where that process was already in place.

## The number that changed the project

We pulled the public gallery and coded 429 postcards from missions NS 30 through NS 37. All four of us went through them separately, tagged what we saw, and clustered the results together.

Two kinds of patterns started to show up. What kids do: handwritten text in 27.2% of postcards, colouring in a single-colour background by hand in 21.0%, colouring inside a shape in 13.5%, trying to blend colours in 11.0%. And what kids draw: planets in 18.4%, stars and dots in 15.4%, rockets in 12.4%.

And then there was 12.9%: postcards that were blank, or barely started. That was the number we kept coming back to.

**It would have been easy to read this as a tooling gap.** In places, it was one. Kids were colouring in whole backgrounds by hand because there was no fill bucket, and writing letters by hand because there was no text tool. You can see them working around what was missing.

This is where it started to come together for us. A fill bucket would help the 21% who had already decided to make something, but it wouldn't reach the 12.9% who opened the page, saw a white rectangle, and left. Once we treated the blank rate as people leaving rather than people missing tools, our attention moved to the first few seconds, before the canvas even loads.

## Five decisions, and the trade-offs behind each one

Each feature traces back to something we learned, and so does each thing we chose to give up.

<Decision num="01" title="You pick a background before you ever see the canvas">
  <Fragment slot="finding">12.9% blank, and 21.0% of the finished ones were a hand-coloured single-colour background. Kids were spending their first burst of effort filling in a rectangle.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Make choosing a background the first step, so nobody starts on an empty white page.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">A blank canvas plus a fill tool. Also a guided tutorial, for reasons the next decision explains.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">If you do want a plain white page, you now have to choose it. We felt that was worth it for the 12.9%.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="02" title="We built an onboarding tutorial, then decided to remove it">
  <Fragment slot="finding">Every participant skipped the instructions, in both rounds of testing. Younger kids didn't notice the small body text. Older kids tried to read it and said it was too small.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Remove the tutorial. Make the text bigger, and let big high-contrast buttons do the explaining instead.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Rewriting the copy in simpler words. Reading level wasn't the issue, since the text wasn't being read.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">Anything that truly needs words now has less room. The privacy and legal copy still lives on the acknowledgement screen, and it's still being skipped.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="03" title="The sticker set came from the gallery">
  <Fragment slot="finding">Planets 18.4%, stars 15.4%, rockets 12.4%. Kids had already shown us what they wanted to draw, using nothing more than a thin pen.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Build those three first, and make them movable objects rather than stamps, so you can place one and still change your mind.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">A large general sticker library with animals, faces and objects.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">If you want to draw something that isn't about space, you get noticeably less help. We made that trade-off on purpose.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="04" title="A trash can icon read as delete">
  <Fragment slot="finding">We used a trash can for "Restart". Participant 2 pointed it out without being asked. Participant 3 looked back and forth between "Background" and "Restart" before choosing. The pan tool confused Participant 1, and the prompt shuffle button confused both six-year-olds and the eight-year-old.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Rename it "Clear", redraw the icon, and take the pan tool out of the toolbar.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Keeping the icon and adding a tooltip. Kids don't hover, and a kiosk doesn't have a cursor to hover with.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">Clearing still wipes everything, and it's still one tap away. We didn't get to designing the confirmation or undo that should sit behind it.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="05" title="We proposed a different survey question to the sponsor">
  <Fragment slot="finding">The sponsor's survey used a standard NPS question: "How likely are you to recommend this program to a friend?" In testing, two participants skipped the survey entirely and a third chose the happiest emoji for reasons unrelated to the question.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Suggest "Did this app make you more excited about space?" instead. Yes or no, two thumbs. The sponsor agreed.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Keeping NPS with easier wording. A scale question a kid answers at random can be worse than no data, because it still looks like data.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">The sponsor gives up a number they could compare across programs, in exchange for one that's meaningful within this one. Our case was that a real yes tells you more than an 8 out of 10 picked at random.</Fragment>
</Decision>

## The space theme wasn't coming through

One of the most surprising findings from round one had nothing to do with tools. Three of four kids either didn't pick up that this was about space, or felt it should be much more obvious. Participant 4 put it best: it needed *"more rocket and stuff, like everywhere, so you know it's about space."*

That surprised us, for a product built around sending your drawing on a rocket. Then it connected with what we'd already seen in testing: the space story lived in the copy, and the copy was the part kids were skipping.

At Engineering Discovery Days we set the prototype up on a touchscreen next to iPads running the current site, and handed out sticky notes. Over a hundred came back. Send-to-space was the most loved part of the whole design. Pre-made backgrounds came second, with a few kids saying they helped when they weren't confident drawing. The fill tool was the most requested missing feature, named four separate times.

**We were careful not to count the booth as solving the space problem, though.** Kids at an engineering fair are already excited about rockets, and the design still needed to work in a quiet museum with nobody there to explain it. So we noted that and kept the question open.

The answer came from rethinking the confirmation screen. Our first version was a dense page of mission stats and crew bios above the survey. It didn't help with the theme, and it added a new problem: mission data changes depending on when you submit, so it needed a live database the sponsor didn't want to maintain.

We proposed an animated launch instead, mocked up as still frames. Your postcard rides through liftoff, stage separation, fairing jettison and landing burn. No words, works for a six-year-old, and no database.

In round three, with kids returning from earlier sessions, the reaction was immediate.

<Pull attr="A returning participant, seeing the launch animation for the first time">
  WOW this is super cool.
</Pull>

They told us they felt more connected to space than they had with the earlier version. A short animation got across what the copy hadn't, and it was exciting to see the research lead somewhere that clear.

## Giving museums a reason to partner

The other half of the brief was institutional. Museums and science centres had no way to brand or customise any of this, which made partnering less appealing.

We built co-branding around one link per partner. The link sets the branding and tracks which submissions came from that location. A partner sends a logo, an optional custom prompt, and any extra artwork, and it shows up in four places: next to the Club for the Future mark during onboarding, as the first suggested background, as the first sticker in the tray, and on the canvas itself.

That last one is my favourite part. Our first attempt laid the logo flat across the artboard with a white outline so it stayed readable, and it looked like a watermark on a child's drawing. Turning it into a postage stamp solved both problems at once. It stays visible, which matters to the partner, and it no longer competes with the artwork, because a stamp in the corner of a postcard is right where a stamp belongs.

<Figure src="/images/postcards/cobrand-stamp.webp" alt="Canvas showing the partner logo rendered as a postage stamp in the upper right corner of the artboard" lead="Partner branding as a stamp.">
  Always visible, can't be drawn over, and it looks like it belongs there.
</Figure>

## Three screen sizes, one set of variables

The sponsor shared seven breakpoints and planned to handle the rest in build. We designed three: kiosk at 1920 by 1080 because that's what stands in the museum, desktop and tablet because that's what you'd use at home, and mobile because it's the only vertical layout and needed the most rethinking.

Instead of making three copies of every component, we put font sizes, padding, margins and dimensions into Figma as numeric variables and set each breakpoint up as a mode. Switch the mode and everything resizes at once. It was faster to work with, and much easier for whoever builds it to read.

<div class="caveat">
  <h2 style="margin-block-start:0">What I'd be careful with in this research</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><b>Round one was four kids we found through people we know.</b> Ages six to twelve, no prior exposure, consent sorted. It caught real problems and I'd run it again, but four kids recruited that way is a signal rather than a sample, and I'd present it that way.</li>
    <li><b>Engineering Discovery Days is a friendly room.</b> Kids at an engineering fair are primed to love rockets. We got great volume and clear direction from it, but it told us less about whether the space theme landed than the enthusiasm might suggest. We noted that in the report.</li>
    <li><b>Two of our gallery categories were hard to pin down.</b> "Colouring inside a shape" and "trying to blend colours" were difficult for four people to code consistently. The headline numbers hold up. Those two I'd call approximate.</li>
    <li><b>None of this measures the program's bigger goal.</b> The program exists to get kids interested in STEM and aerospace careers, and we measured one session at a kiosk. Whether that interest lasts beyond the museum visit is the question I'd most like to explore next, and it was beyond what ten weeks could answer.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

**What we handed over:** a high-fidelity Figma prototype in basic and co-branded versions, across kiosk, desktop, tablet and mobile, for Club for the Future to build from. Every major feature traces back to something we found or something we tested. None of it has shipped to the public platform.

**What I'd do differently:** run the gallery analysis before the expert interviews instead of alongside them. That 12.9% turned out to be our most useful finding, and we found it in week three. If we'd gone into the conversations with Yip and Kientz already holding it, we could have spent that time on why kids were leaving, rather than on narrowing the age range, which existing research could have helped us with.
