## The brief said K-12. That lasted about a week.

Club for the Future lets K-12 students draw a digital postcard. It flies to space on a New Shepard or New Glenn rocket, comes back with a "Flown to Space" stamp on it, and lands in your inbox. It runs on the website and on touchscreen kiosks in museums, and thousands of kids have done it.

We took the brief at face value and started designing. Two expert interviews put a stop to that. Jason Yip from KidsTeam at the UW iSchool, and Julie Kientz, who chairs our department, both said the same thing without talking to each other: a six-year-old and a seventeen-year-old have nothing in common. Design for both at once and you'll please neither.

Kientz also gave us the line that ended up shaping the whole look of the thing.

<Pull attr="Julie Kientz, Chair, UW Human Centered Design & Engineering">
  Kids prefer interfaces that don't feel like they were made for younger kids, even when they are.
</Pull>

So we aimed at 8 to 12 and kept the edges covered with a low floor and a high ceiling. Easy to start, plenty of depth if you go looking for it.

Yip also raised the thing we hadn't thought about at all: doing research with kids under a corporate sponsor is a consent problem before it's a design problem. Kientz pointed us at UW Engineering Discovery Days, where that part was already sorted.

## The number that changed the project

We pulled the public gallery and coded 429 postcards from missions NS 30 through NS 37. All four of us went through them separately, tagged what we saw, and clustered the results.

Two things fell out. What kids do: handwritten text in 27.2% of postcards, colouring in a single-colour background by hand in 21.0%, colouring inside a shape in 13.5%, trying to blend colours in 11.0%. And what kids draw: planets in 18.4%, stars and dots in 15.4%, rockets in 12.4%.

Then there was 12.9%. Blank, or barely started.

**The easy read is that the tools were too thin.** And honestly, they were. Kids were hand-filling entire backgrounds one pixel at a time because there was no fill bucket, and drawing letters by hand because there was no text tool. You can see them working around the gaps.

But that read only gets you halfway. A fill bucket helps the 21% who already decided to make something. It does nothing for the 12.9% who opened the page, saw a white rectangle, and closed it. So we treated the blank rate as people leaving rather than people lacking tools, which moved everything upstream into the seconds before the canvas even loads.

## Five decisions, and what each one cost us

Every feature here traces back to something we found. So does everything we gave up to get it.

<Decision num="01" title="You pick a background before you ever see the canvas">
  <Fragment slot="finding">12.9% blank, and 21.0% of the finished ones were a hand-coloured single-colour background. Kids were spending their first burst of effort filling in a rectangle.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Make choosing a background the first step, so nobody ever meets an empty white page.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Blank canvas plus a fill tool. Also a guided tutorial, for reasons the next card explains.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">If you actually wanted a plain white page, you now have to choose it. We took that trade for the 12.9%.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="02" title="We built an onboarding tutorial, then deleted it">
  <Fragment slot="finding">Every single participant skipped the instructions, in both rounds of testing. The younger ones didn't even register small body text. The older ones tried to read it and said it was too small.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Cut the tutorial. Make the text bigger, and let big high-contrast buttons do the explaining instead.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Rewriting the copy in simpler words. The reading level wasn't the issue. Nobody was reading it at all.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">Anything that genuinely needs words now has nowhere to go. The privacy and legal copy is still sitting on the acknowledgement screen, still getting skipped.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="03" title="The sticker set came from the gallery, not from us">
  <Fragment slot="finding">Planets 18.4%, stars 15.4%, rockets 12.4%. Kids had already told us what they wanted to draw by drawing it badly with a one-pixel pen.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Build those three first, and make them movable objects rather than stamps, so you can put one down and still change your mind.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">A big general sticker library with animals and faces and objects.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">If you want to draw something that isn't about space, you get noticeably less help. That's a real narrowing and we did it on purpose.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="04" title="The trash can meant delete">
  <Fragment slot="finding">We used a trash can for "Restart". Participant 2 called it out without being asked. Participant 3 looked back and forth between "Background" and "Restart" before guessing. The pan tool baffled Participant 1, and the prompt shuffle button confused both six-year-olds and the eight-year-old.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Rename it "Clear", redraw the icon, and pull the pan tool out of the toolbar entirely.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Keeping the icon and adding a tooltip. Kids don't hover, and a kiosk doesn't have a cursor to hover with.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">Clearing still wipes everything and it's still one tap away. We never designed the "are you sure" or the undo that should sit behind it.</Fragment>
</Decision>

<Decision num="05" title="We talked the sponsor out of their own survey question">
  <Fragment slot="finding">The sponsor wanted a standard NPS question: "How likely are you to recommend this program to a friend?" In testing, two participants skipped the survey entirely and a third picked the happiest emoji for no reason related to the question.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="did">Swap it for "Did this app make you more excited about space?" Yes or no, two thumbs.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="rejected">Keeping NPS with easier wording. A scale question a kid answers at random is worse than no data, because it still looks like data.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="cost">The sponsor loses a number they could compare against other programs, and gains one that only means anything inside this one. We argued a real yes beats a made-up 8 out of 10.</Fragment>
</Decision>

## Nobody noticed it was about space

The most awkward finding from round one had nothing to do with tools. Three of four kids either didn't pick up that this was about space, or thought it should be way more obvious. Participant 4 put it best: it needed *"more rocket and stuff, like everywhere, so you know it's about space."*

Which is a strange problem for a product whose entire pitch is that your drawing goes on a rocket. The pitch was in the copy. The copy was the part nobody read.

At Engineering Discovery Days we set the prototype up on a touchscreen next to iPads running the current site, and handed out sticky notes. Over a hundred came back. Send-to-space was the most loved thing in the whole design. Pre-made backgrounds came second, with a few kids saying it helped when they weren't confident drawing. The fill tool was the most asked-for missing feature, named four separate times.

**We didn't count the booth as solving the space problem, though.** It's an engineering fair. Everyone there already likes rockets. The thing still had to work in a quiet museum with nobody standing next to it, so we wrote that down and left the problem open.

What actually closed it was throwing out the confirmation screen. Version one was a wall of mission stats and crew bios sitting on top of the survey. It solved nothing, and it created a new problem: mission data changes depending on when you submit, so it needed a live database the sponsor didn't want to run.

We pitched an animated launch instead, mocked up as still frames. Your postcard rides through liftoff, stage separation, fairing jettison, landing burn. No words, works for a six-year-old, no database.

Round three, with kids coming back from earlier sessions, the reaction was instant.

<Pull attr="A returning participant, seeing the launch animation for the first time">
  WOW this is super cool.
</Pull>

They told us they felt more connected to space than they had with the earlier version. Twelve seconds of motion did what three rounds of copy couldn't.

## Giving museums a reason to care

The other half of the brief was institutional. Museums and science centres had no way to brand or customise any of this, which made it a weak thing to partner on.

We built co-branding around one link per partner. The link sets the branding and tracks which submissions came from that location. A partner sends a logo, an optional custom prompt, and any extra artwork, and it shows up in four places: next to the Club for the Future mark during onboarding, as the first suggested background, as the first sticker in the tray, and on the canvas itself.

That last one is the bit I like. Our first attempt laid the logo flat across the artboard with a white outline so it stayed readable, and it looked exactly like a watermark on a child's drawing. Turning it into a postage stamp fixed both problems at once. It stays visible, which is what the partner is paying for, and it stops fighting the artwork, because a stamp in the corner of a postcard is just where a stamp goes.

<Figure src="/images/postcards/cobrand-stamp.webp" alt="Canvas showing the partner logo rendered as a postage stamp in the upper right corner of the artboard" lead="Partner branding as a stamp.">
  Always visible, can't be drawn over, and it looks like it belongs there.
</Figure>

## Three screen sizes, one set of variables

The sponsor handed us seven breakpoints and said they'd handle the rest in build. We did three: kiosk at 1920 by 1080 because that's what stands in the museum, desktop and tablet because that's what you'd use at home, and mobile because it's the only vertical one and therefore the awkward one.

Instead of making three copies of every component, we put font sizes, padding, margins and dimensions into Figma as numeric variables and set each breakpoint up as a mode. Switch the mode and everything resizes at once. Faster to work with, and much easier for whoever builds it to read.

<div class="caveat">
  <h2 style="margin-block-start:0">What I don't trust about this research</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><b>Round one was four kids we found through people we know.</b> Ages six to twelve, no prior exposure, consent sorted. It caught real problems and I'd run it again, but four kids recruited that way is a signal, not a sample, and I wouldn't present it as one.</li>
    <li><b>Engineering Discovery Days is a friendly room.</b> Kids at an engineering fair are primed to love rockets. We got great volume and clear direction out of it, and it told us less about whether the space theme landed than the enthusiasm suggested. We said so in the report instead of banking the win.</li>
    <li><b>Two of our gallery categories were fuzzy.</b> "Colouring inside a shape" and "trying to blend colours" were genuinely hard for four people to judge the same way. The headline numbers hold up. Those two I'd call approximate.</li>
    <li><b>None of this measures the actual goal.</b> The programme exists to get kids interested in STEM and aerospace careers. We measured one session at a kiosk. Whether any of it survives the walk out of the museum is the question I'd want to answer next, and ten weeks was never going to get there.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

**What we handed over:** a high-fidelity Figma prototype in basic and co-branded versions, across kiosk, desktop, tablet and mobile, for Club for the Future to build from. Every major feature traces back to something we found or something we tested. None of it has shipped to the public platform.

**What I'd do differently:** run the gallery analysis before the expert interviews instead of alongside them. That 12.9% was the best thing we had and we found it in week three. If we'd walked into the Yip and Kientz conversations already holding it, we could have spent that time on the drop-off problem instead of on narrowing the age range, which honestly the research papers could have told us.
